Crop Rotation for Better Yields

July 28, 2024•4 min read

How this traditional practice can improve soil fertility and increase overall farm productivity season after season.

Crop rotation is one of the oldest agricultural practices in the world — and one of the most effective. By systematically changing which crops are grown in each field from season to season, farmers can improve soil health, reduce pest and disease pressure, and increase yields without additional inputs.

The Science Behind Crop Rotation

Different crops have different nutrient requirements and different effects on soil. Legumes fix atmospheric nitrogen into the soil, effectively fertilizing it for the next crop. Deep-rooted crops break up compacted soil layers.

  • Legumes can add 50–200 kg of nitrogen per hectare — equivalent to significant fertilizer savings
  • Breaking pest cycles reduces pesticide needs by 30–50% in rotated fields
  • Soil organic matter increases by 0.1–0.3% annually in well-rotated systems
  • Weed pressure decreases as different crops compete differently with weed species

A Simple Rotation Plan for Nepal

A basic 3-year rotation suitable for most Nepali vegetable farms: Year 1 — heavy feeders (tomatoes, cabbage, corn); Year 2 — legumes (beans, peas, lentils); Year 3 — root vegetables (carrots, radish, potato). This simple sequence breaks pest cycles, replenishes nitrogen, and maintains soil structure.

For rice-based farming systems in the Terai, alternating rice with legumes like lentils or mustard during the dry season is a proven approach that improves both soil health and farm income.

Planning Your Rotation

Effective rotation requires planning ahead — knowing what you will grow in each field for the next 2–3 seasons. Krishi Connect's farm management tools include a simple rotation planner that tracks field history and suggests optimal next crops based on what was previously grown and current market demand.

Common Mistakes to Avoid

The most common mistake is rotating within the same plant family — for example, following tomatoes with peppers or potatoes, all of which are in the nightshade family and share the same pests and diseases. Effective rotation requires moving to a completely different plant family each season.
